1. Introduction
The persistence of global current account imbalances has been an important policy consideration for policymakers and academics alike, especially those in developed economies. Whether it be after the Great Recession in 2008, during the Euro Crisis between 2010 and 2012, or in the current era of geoeconomic fragmentation, economists and policymakers have long expressed concerns of the persistence current account imbalances. These concerns have at times permeated the financial markets, leading to credit downgrades or speculative attacks on government bonds. This is not just a developing country issue, with Moody's downgrading the United States' credit rating in May of 2025. These concerns can, in turn, lead to fiscal policy constraints, limiting the ability of countries to implement fiscal stimulus when needed. In particular, maintaining current account surpluses and accumulating a strategic stock of international reserves in the past is associated with better resilience during monetary tightenings (Aizenman et al. 2024, 2025). A similar line of reasoning can be formulated for episodes of financial stress. During the Great Recession in 2008 and the Pandemic Recession in 2020, many countries sought international bailouts to maintain solvency or used swap lines to ensure short-term liquidity. Yet, while these countries struggled to meet their debt obligations, others amassed savings and more immune to financial turmoil.
Once again after the post-Great Recession correction, the current account imbalances are growing with the IMF (2025) reporting a global current account widening of 0.6 percentage points of world GDP. Before the Great Recession, there has been a consistent and recurring profligacy of several advanced economies, including the US, being financed by excess savings of emerging market economies, most notably the People’s Republic of China (PRC), and oil-exporting countries. Imbalanced capital flows have enabled some countries to run persistent and massive current account deficits, financed by the persistent current account surpluses of other countries. Researchers, such as Chinn (2011), have investigated the causes of global imbalances and found that many factors are intricately intertwined, creating 'uphill' flows of excess savings from developing countries with high rates of return to developed countries with lower rates of return but more advanced financial markets (the 'Lucas paradox', Lucas (1990)). However, the global financial crisis and the European debt crisis revealed that the world economy is on a delicate balancing act regarding imbalances in capital flows. A change in global economic conditions can suddenly alter the direction of capital flows, potentially disrupting real economies. On the other hand, persistent capital flows can put the world economy in a crisis-prone situation like that of the pre-crisis period. Given this unstable environment, examining the country-specific determinants of persistent current account deficits or surpluses can provide deeper insights about the understanding of the growing global imbalances and the stability of the international financial system.
In theory, current account balances of a country should evolve in a way that satisfies the long-run intertemporal national budget constraint (LRBC). In reference to Feldstein (1980), Taylor (2002) argues that the LRBC implies that savings and investment must be highly correlated as countries approach long-run steady-state. This does not preclude short-run deviations from the LRBC, however, as these can be caused by macroeconomic and institutional policy changes related to savings and investment.
It is difficult to draw conclusions on current account sustainability from past literature, however, due to inconsistent or, at best, inconclusive evidence on the sustainability of current account balances. This may, in part, arise from inconsistencies in testing methodologies. Alternatively, it may reflect a failure to appropriately distinguish long-run sustainability from short-run dynamics. As Taylor (2002) argues, even within the context of the LRBC, countries may run 'unsustainable' current account imbalances for short periods without necessarily violating the long-run constraint. Hence, it is important to distinguish long-run sustainability from short-run deviations from the intertemporal constraint and not falsely reject long-run current account sustainability based on evidence of short-run non-stationarity of current account balances. 
Once current account balances are found to be mean-reverting, either globally or locally, the degree of current account persistency can vary not only across countries but also over time. As we will show later, in the period leading up to the Great Recession, both current account surplus and deficit countries experienced persistent current account imbalances. Persistent current account imbalances do not necessarily lead to immediate concerns about external debt sustainability, as the speed of reversion can differ across countries and time periods, depending on their macroeconomic policies and institutional characteristics. Before the Great Recession, the PRC’s persistent current account surplus and its quasi-fixed exchange rate policy have raised questions about whether and how exchange rate regimes affect current account persistency. Chinn and Wei (2013) investigated this issue and found no significant or systematic relationship between exchange rate regimes and the degree of current account persistency, contrary to the common anecdotal belief that flexible exchange rates should lead to current account adjustments. Beyond exchange rate regimes, it is important to investigate the fundamentals that contribute to different degrees of current account persistence.
Given this background, this paper will examine the dynamics of current account balances leveraging a large quarterly dataset for current account balances, focusing particularly on their persistence and the factors that determine it. First, we will re-examine the stationarity of current account balances for 77 countries. Several stationarity tests using standard linear unit root procedures confirm that the time series of current account balances (as a share of GDP) are not stationary for many countries, contrary to theoretical predictions. Second, we will investigate whether the lack of statistical evidence for the stationarity of current account balances is driven by regime shifts in their time series, following an ongoing strand of literature that tests nonlinearity in current account persistence using Markov-Switching models (Taylor, 2002; Raybaudi et al., 2004; Chen, 2011; Poulakis & Krykilis, 2024). Finally, we will examine whether the probability of entering a non-stationary current account regime, as well as the degree of persistence across different regimes, can be explained by cross-sectional and temporal variations in policies, institutions, and macroeconomic fundamentals.

Despite theoretical predictions that a country must obey the LRBC and that current account balances must be long-run stationarity processes, empirical unit root tests have varied success supporting this conclusion. 3.2. Markov-Switching Estimation
We document changes in persistence, volatility, and mean across regimes and investigate whether current account balances exhibit local or global unit roots. Our empirical strategy follows the Markov-switching framework proposed by Morita et al. As emphasized by Morita et al. (2023), two features of this specification are crucial for robust unit-root inference. First, allowing the mean, variance, and persistence to switch improves reliability because failing to model changes in mean or volatility may cause these shifts to be absorbed into persistence estimates, thereby generating spurious unit-root results (Shi 2012; Harvey et al. 2016; Monschauer and Wilfling 2021; Morita et al. 2024). Second, the regime shifts in mean, variance, and persistence are driven by independent Markov chains. This independence allows us to distinguish shifts in persistence from changes in mean or volatility. For instance, an explosive-persistence regime may occur regardless of whether volatility is high or low.

3.. Global Stationarity
A key advantage of this framework is its ability to distinguish local stationarity, which describes the behavior within a given regime, from global stationarity, which concerns the stability of the full Markov-switching process. Local stationarity alone is not sufficient for assessing long-run sustainability because transitions across regimes also affect the global dynamics.

Even if each regime is locally stationary, these inequalities may fail, implying that the overall current account process is globally non-stationary. For example, current account balances may appear mean reverting within each regime, yet frequent transitions between regimes may prevent convergence to a stable long run distribution.
The Markov-switching approach delivers an empirical counterpart to the core theoretical insight of the intertemporal model: external sustainability requires that the current account does not follow a path that violates the long run budget constraint. In theory, this condition appears through the transversality requirement, which rules out ever-increasing foreign liabilities and ensures that the country’s intertemporal budget remains balanced. In practice, however, countries may go through periods of temporary overheating, sharp reversals, or structural breaks. These shifts make a single-regime unit-root test poorly suited to capturing the dynamics implied by the theory.
The Markov-switching model provides a way to reconcile these theoretical requirements with the empirical reality of current account dynamics. By allowing the series to alternate between stationary, unit-root, and explosive regimes, the model acknowledges that countries may temporarily experience explosive behavior, for example during crises or episodes of sudden stops. What matters for sustainability is not the absence of such episodes, but their frequency and persistence. The regime transition probabilities therefore play a central role: they tell us whether the economy tends to return to stable dynamics quickly enough to be consistent with the long run intertemporal constraint.
Global stationarity in this framework has a direct theoretical interpretation. When the combined effect of persistence within each regime and the probability of remaining in those regimes satisfies the Psaradakis et al. (2004) conditions, the overall process behaves in a way that is compatible with the long-term budget constraint. This means that even if the current account occasionally enters an explosive regime, the system does not drift indefinitely away from equilibrium. The Bohn-type sustainability logic is naturally embedded here: the model allows the current account to react differently to the accumulation of external positions across regimes, and global stationarity requires a sufficiently strong corrective mechanism over the long run.
In this sense, the Markov-switching approach does more than simply classify regimes: it offers a rigorous way to assess whether the observed behavior of the current account is consistent with the theoretical foundations of external sustainability. It captures the fact that countries live through structural change, crises, and adjustments while still being subject to the intertemporal budget constraint that governs their long-run external position.

1. Core Objective and Framing
What the paper claims/does
The paper studies “the statistical nature of the persistency of current account balances and its determinants” for 70 countries, with implications for global rebalancing. 
It assumes that a stationary current account series ensures the long-run budget constraint but allows that countries may experience “local non-stationarity” (MS regimes) along the way. 
It uses a Markov-switching ADF framework to identify stationary vs locally non-stationary regimes and dates of structural shifts, then studies the determinants of both:
Probit on the probability of entering a non-stationary (“random walk”) regime. 
OLS on the persistence (AR coefficient) within stationary 
Key empirical findings (as summarized in the abstract and Section 4):
Fixed exchange rate regimes and greater financial openness for emerging markets increase the probability of entering a random-walk (non-stationary) regime. 
Trade openness reduces the likelihood of entering the non-stationary regime and reduces persistence. 
Budget deficits and current account deficit regimes are associated with staying in stationary regimes (interpreted as markets forcing rebalancing). 
When looking at persistence within stationary regimes, exchange rate regime dummies no longer matter, but openness and RER misalignment do. 
What referees say
They broadly accept the descriptive goal (characterizing persistence and breaks) and find the empirical patterns “interesting.” 
But they question whether the paper’s framing around sustainability and global rebalancing is justified by the econometrics.

2. Stationarity, Persistence, and Sustainability / LRBC
Paper’s stance
The abstract and introduction tie stationarity of the current account to satisfaction of the long-run intertemporal budget constraint, and discuss non-stationarity (particularly for deficit countries) as a potential “red signal” for LRBC violations or unsustainable imbalances. 
Referee reaction
JIE Reviewer 1 and 2, and JIMF Reviewer 2 all emphasize that:
Stationarity is sufficient but not necessary for the LRBC or sustainability. They explicitly reference Bohn-type results: as long as a relevant debt variable is stationary after finite differencing, the LRBC can be satisfied even if the CA itself is non-stationary. 
Reviewer 1 gives a formal example where the current account is non-stationary but the LRBC holds, and recommends downplaying the interpretation of CA non-stationarity as evidence of unsustainability. 
JIMF Reviewer 2 is particularly clear:
Historical stationarity of the CA does not tell us sustainability “at present or at any particular point in the past.”
The data do not explicitly account for crises or defaults, so stationarity/non-stationarity of the series is at best loosely linked to sustainability.
Bottom line:
The paper uses stationarity and persistence as empirical proxies for sustainability and rebalancing pressure; referees accept that there is some connection but think the equivalence is overstated and needs theoretical clarification and downscaling.
3. Markov-Switching ADF and Identification of Regimes
Paper’s methodology and claims
Section 3.2 uses a Markov-Switching ADF test (Raybaudi et al.; Chen) to distinguish between:
A random walk regime (state 0)
A mean-reverting AR(1) regime (state 1)
with regime-dependent constant and AR coefficient. 
The aim is to:
Improve rejection rates of unit roots compared to linear tests.
Identify locally non-stationary episodes (random-walk regimes) and global stationarity conditions (via second-order conditions). 
Regime classifications and episode dates are then used for:
Table of non-stationary episodes (e.g., China 2008, U.S. long deficit episode). 
Construction of the dependent variables for the probit and persistence regressions. 

Referee critiques
“Mechanical” identification concern:
JIE Reviewer 2 calls MS-ADF “a mechanical solution to improve the rejection rates of unit roots,” and questions whether the identified breaks correspond to economically meaningful shifts. 
They ask directly how MS-identified shifts compare to Chinn & Wei’s regime-based AR shifts and whether the two line up. 
Episode plausibility:
JIE Reviewer 2 questions episodes like China in 2008, noting that the four quarters flagged as locally non-stationary are not obviously special, and thus the classification “raises more questions than provides support.” 
Mapping & sampling issues (JIMF):
JIMF referees raise concerns about how the quarterly MS regimes are mapped to annual data for the probit, and suggest considering MS models estimated directly on annual data for comparability and robustness. 
They also worry about outliers (China, U.S.) and whether regime classification is robust for these.
Bottom line:
The MS-ADF setup is central to the paper. Referees accept its technical legitimacy but doubt:
whether it truly identifies “non-stationary regimes” in an economically meaningful way, and
whether the improved rejection rates translate into substantive insight about sustainability or crises.
4. Determinants of Non-Stationary Regimes (Probit) and Persistence (OLS)
Paper’s approach
Section 4:
Probit: dependent variable = indicator for being in a locally non-stationary regime in a given year. Explanatory variables include exchange rate regime, RER misalignment, financial development, financial and trade openness, income level, reserves, NFA, budget balance, CA size, exporter dummies, crisis dummy, euro dummy, etc. 
OLS: persistence measured by the AR coefficient from regimes, regressed on similar fundamentals. 
Findings:
Fixed ER increases the probability of entering the random-walk regime for developing/emerging markets; trade openness and NFA reduce that probability; financial openness can increase it for EMEs. 
Within stationary regimes, exchange rate regime matters less; openness and RER misalignment reduce persistence; reserves increase it. 
Referee critiques
“Kitchen-sink” criticism (JIE Reviewer 1):
The determinants section (especially the long list of controls) is described as having a “kitchen-sink” feel with no clear underlying theoretical framework being tested. 
Suggestion: narrow the focus to a core variable (e.g., exchange rate regime) with strong theory, and treat the rest as supporting controls with rigorous robustness checks. 
Inconsistent signs and interpretation:
The JIE referee notes that variables like capital account openness flip sign and significance between the probit and OLS results (positive in entering non-stationary regimes, negative for persistence in stationary regimes), making the economic interpretation unclear. 
Omitted variable / exogeneity concerns:
JIE Reviewers and JIMF Reviewers question treating exchange rate regime, RER misalignment, and reserve accumulation as exogenous to CA persistence, and also stress possible multicollinearity and interaction effects (e.g., regime dummies vs RER misalignment). 
Contribution vs existing literature:
JIE Reviewer 2 notes that effects of trade openness, financial development, etc., are already well documented; the value-added isn’t clear given how much of Section 4 revisits these known correlates. 

Bottom line:
The determinants section is seen as empirically rich but theoretically under-anchored, with too many variables and some conflicting patterns, making it hard for referees to extract a crisp economic message.

5. Exchange Rate Regimes: The Key “New” Result
Paper’s claim
Abstract and conclusions emphasize an important, seemingly novel result:
Emerging market countries with fixed exchange rate regimes are more likely to enter a random-walk (non-stationary) regime, i.e., fixed ERs are associated with more persistent or non-rebalancing CA behavior, in contrast to Chinn & Wei. 

Referee reaction
They recognize this as the only clearly new result relative to the literature—but also the most troubling:
JIE Reviewer 2: for results that match existing literature, we don’t learn much new; for the single divergent result (role of ER regime), the paper does not explain well why it differs from Chinn & Wei. 
They ask whether differences arise from probit vs mean-reverting regressions, from sample, from regime definition, or from non-linearity. 
They also note that your binary regime classification (fixed vs flexible) aggregates categories that Chinn & Wei treat separately (float, dirty float, crawl, etc.), possibly washing out the non-linearities they find. 
In OLS persistence regressions exchange rate regimes no longer matter, which they feel is awkward given the storyline built around ER regimes. 

Bottom line:
Referees see the ER-regime result as potentially important if convincingly justified—but currently under-explained and somewhat fragile, especially relative to Chinn & Wei.

6. Data and Construction Issues
Paper
Uses quarterly CA data and MS-ADF to identify regimes, then aggregates to annual values for probit and other regressions. 
Constructs many explanatory variables as:
3-year sums or averages (e.g., CA size, budget balance, financial development) and deviations from world means (financial openness, reserves). 
Referees
Ask for clarity on the mapping from quarterly MS regimes to annual probit data and suggest exploring MS models estimated directly on annual series. 
Question the logic of using deviations from the world mean (e.g., for financial openness) and ask for more explanation. 
Raise concerns about outlier countries (China, U.S. episodes) and whether the regime identification for them is robust and economically interpretable. 

7. Exposition, Narrative, and Policy Language
Paper
The introduction links global imbalances, CA persistence, and crisis risk, sometimes with strong language (e.g., “severe constraints,” “delicate balancing act,” “red signal”). 

Referees
JIMF Reviewer 1 criticizes some macro statements:
Questions describing the U.S. as facing “severe constraints on fiscal policy” and sees the “delicate balancing act” phrase as overstated. 
Finds the explanation of the crisis impact on imbalances (via nominal GDP changes) unconvincing. 
Both sets of referees suggest that the policy/sustainability implications should be toned down or more carefully qualified, and that the introduction/conclusion should more modestly reflect what the econometric evidence can actually support. 

8. Overall Alignment vs. Tension
Putting it all together:
Aligned with referees:
The paper’s descriptive findings on structural breaks, CA persistence, and roles of trade and financial openness are largely seen as consistent with existing literature—and often appreciated as interesting and well executed. 

Main points of tension:
Conceptual link between stationarity and sustainability/LRBC is stronger in the paper than referees think is defensible.
MS-ADF regime identification is central to the contribution, but referees question whether the resulting regimes and breaks are economically meaningful, and whether the improved rejection rate really “solves” anything.
Determinants section is empirically ambitious but perceived as too broad, under-theorized, and somewhat internally inconsistent.
The exchange-rate-regime result is recognized as the key novelty but not yet convincingly reconciled with earlier work or fully supported by robustness and theory.
The policy and sustainability rhetoric (global rebalancing, red signals, constraints) is seen as stronger than what the empirical design can sustain.

Reframing Strategy to Improve the Paper’s Publishability
1. Shift the Core Contribution Away From “Sustainability” Toward “Dynamics”
Why?
Across both JIE and JIMF reviews, the most consistent criticism was:
“Stationarity of the current account does not imply sustainability.”
“The conceptual link to LRBC is overstated.”

Reframe as:
“An empirical characterization of the dynamic adjustment of current account balances in the presence of structural breaks, using nonlinear time-series methods.”
This framing:
Removes the sustainability overreach.
Places your paper firmly within international macro measurement and dynamics, where MS approaches are valued.
Emphasizes what the referees consistently liked:
the documentation of structural breaks and persistence patterns.

2. Reposition the Markov-Switching Contribution
Original framing:
MS-ADF tests are introduced as a solution to the unit-root problem and as a way to diagnose sustainability.

Referees’ concerns:
Mechanically improved rejection rates do not imply deeper understanding.
Identified breaks must be meaningful economically.

Reframed value proposition:
“This paper contributes a nonlinear, regime-dependent approach to understanding how and when current account adjustment accelerates or slows down, distinguishing persistent vs. mean-reverting phases.”
In this framing:
MS is not a “fix” for unit-root tests.
MS is a window into the evolution of adjustment behavior, not a sustainability test.
Your contribution becomes descriptive and methodological, not normative.
This aligns much better with:
Studies on crisis-predictive dynamics
Current account adjustment literature (Taylor 2002; Freund 2005; Lane & Milesi-Ferretti)
Nonlinear international macro time-series journals

3. Recast the Exchange Rate Regime Result as the Central, Carefully Explained Contribution
Why?
Referees agree this is your most novel finding but found it under-explained.

Reframe:
“Fixed exchange rate regimes in emerging markets are associated with more persistent current account imbalances because adjustment mechanisms operate differently depending on regime flexibility.”
Then support this reframing with:
Theoretical intuition:
Fixed regimes constrain expenditure-switching → slower CA adjustment → higher persistence.
Literature links:
Recent research on ER rigidity, capital flows, and external adjustment.
A robustness roadmap:
Include alternative regime classifications (e.g., coarse vs. fine Chinn–Ito), regional sensitivity checks, annual-data MS robustness.

This turns your “disputed finding” into a theoretically grounded hypothesis supported by your nonlinear method.

4. Narrow and Strengthen Section 4 (Determinants)
Original problem:
Referees called it “kitchen-sink” and under-theorized.

Reframe as:
(a) One central hypothesis (exchange rate regime),
with
(b) A pruned set of theoretically motivated controls, such as:
trade openness
financial openness
NFA position
crisis indicators
What to remove or de-emphasize:
Variables with ambiguous or reversed signs across models
Variables whose inclusion isn’t clearly grounded in theory
Explanatory variables the referees said are questionable as exogenous (reserves, RER misalignment)
This makes the model cleaner, more rigorous, and easier to defend.

5. Strengthen the Interpretation of “Local Non-Stationarity”

Referee concern:
Non-stationarity ≠ red signal ≠ sustainability problem.

Reframe it as:
“Locally non-stationary regimes represent phases of unusually strong shocks, trend shifts, or policy transitions that temporarily alter the speed of current account adjustment.”

To reinforce this:
Provide economic narratives for major episodes (e.g., China, U.S., crises).
Emphasize transitions rather than violations.
This addresses the referees’ concerns directly while preserving your empirical insights.

“Violating LRBC”
“Severe constraints”

“Implications for understanding adjustment dynamics”

“Shifts in the medium-term trajectory”
This aligns with the empirical nature of your work.
