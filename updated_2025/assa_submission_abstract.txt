ABSTRACT (276 words)
================================================================================

What structural factors drive countries into explosive current account dynamics —
where external imbalances amplify rather than correct? Explosive episodes have
featured prominently in balance-of-payments crises and sudden stops, yet the
structural conditions that predict their onset remain poorly understood. Using a
panel of 74 countries spanning 1971–2023, we apply a two-stage empirical
strategy. In the first stage, country-specific Markov-switching autoregressive
models classify each country-year observation into one of three dynamic regimes —
explosive, unit root, or stationary — allowing persistence, mean, and volatility
to switch independently. In the second stage, a multinomial logit model links
regime membership to structural covariates with cluster-robust inference and
cross-validated model selection. Fixed exchange rate arrangements are associated
with a nine-percentage-point reduction in the probability of explosive dynamics;
manufacturing export specialization and trade openness are associated with
increases. The exchange rate result survives when the regime indicator is lagged
one period to address simultaneity, and is marginally stronger in the lagged
specification — consistent with reverse causality working against the hypothesis.
These effects are concentrated entirely in the explosive-versus-non-explosive
channel: within the stable regime space, structural variables do not reliably
distinguish stationary from unit root behavior. Our exchange rate finding
complements Chinn and Wei (2013), who find no systematic relationship between
regime flexibility and current account adjustment speed in linear models; the
exchange rate regime effect is concentrated in the explosive tail of the
distribution, invisible to linear methods. Model fit is four times stronger for
emerging market economies (McFadden R-squared of 0.19 versus 0.05 for the full
sample), indicating that exchange rate and trade structures are most consequential
for middle-income countries, where institutional constraints on external
adjustment are weakest.
